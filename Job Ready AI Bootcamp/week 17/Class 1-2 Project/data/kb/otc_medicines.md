# Over-the-Counter Medicines

> Educational, fictional clinic handbook; not medical advice.

## What over-the-counter means
Over-the-counter (OTC) medicines can be bought without a prescription, for example some pain relievers, antihistamines and antacids. OTC does not mean risk-free: they can still cause side effects, interact with other medicines or be unsafe for some people.

## Reading a medicine label
Before taking any medicine, read the whole label. Check the active ingredient, what it is for, who should not take it, how much to take, how often, and the maximum amount in 24 hours. Keep the leaflet in case you need to check it again.

## Avoiding a double dose of the same ingredient
Many cold and flu remedies contain the same active ingredient as a pain reliever, often paracetamol. Taking two products with the same ingredient can lead to an accidental overdose. Compare the active ingredients on every product you take, and ask a pharmacist if you are not sure.

## Medicines for children
Use only products made for children, follow the age and weight guidance on the label, and measure liquid medicines with the syringe or spoon that comes with them, not a kitchen spoon. Some adult medicines, such as aspirin, are not suitable for children. Ask a pharmacist before giving any new medicine.

## When to ask before using OTC medicines
Speak to a pharmacist or clinician before using any OTC medicine if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, take regular prescription medicines, have a long-term condition such as kidney, liver or heart disease, or are buying for a baby. Only a clinician who knows your history can advise on doses for you personally.
